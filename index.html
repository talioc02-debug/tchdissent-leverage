Dissent & Leverage

The law behind two headlines: an officer who spoke out in uniform, and a President weighing a deal over the Strait of Hormuz

The U.S. Capitol, Washington D.C. — where Major Watson was arrested. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

01 Before You Begin

Two news reports, broadcast by two outlets that sit at very different points on the American political spectrum, tell one story from two angles: a U.S. officer who risked his career to accuse his Commander-in-Chief, and a President who says he holds the cards in a negotiation over the world’s most important oil chokepoint. You will read them the way a lawyer reads evidence: who says what, on what basis, and with what legal consequences.

In this lesson you will

  • understand the language of U.S. military justice: court-martial, discharge, contemptuous words, lawful orders;
  • master the vocabulary of negotiation and leverage in a live geopolitical and energy context;
  • use reporting verbs and hedging to relay contested claims without adopting them;
  • transfer this language to your own work: calls with U.S. counsel, force-majeure questions, media requests.

Warm-up: reflect before reading

  1. Where would you draw the line between a professional’s duty of loyalty and a duty to speak out? Think of your own organisation’s code of conduct.
  2. Have you ever negotiated when the other side clearly held more leverage? What did you do?
  3. When a conflict disrupts shipping and energy markets, which legal risks come to your mind first?
🔑 Teacher note. Suggested timing (≈ 2 h 15 min): warm-up 10′ · Reading 1 + 1B with audio 30′ · Reading 2 + “Read Like a Lawyer” 25′ · vocabulary + memory game 15′ · Exercises 1–5: 30′ · Exercises 6–7: 20′ · Exercise 8 (in class or as homework) 15′ · wrap-up 5′.

Sample warm-up answers: (1) “I’d speak out internally first, through compliance or the board, and go public only where there is a legal duty or a serious risk to safety.” (2) “We had a single supplier; we bought time, developed an alternative and came back with a credible BATNA.” (3) “Force majeure and hardship clauses, war-risk insurance, sanctions compliance, price-adjustment mechanisms.”
🔑 Teacher note. Handling the content. The topic is politically charged. The lesson treats both broadcasts as texts to analyse, not positions to endorse. Keep the focus on language, legal mechanics and source analysis; the student may hold any view, and the writing task is assessed on language, not on position.

02 Reading 1 · The Officer on the Capitol Steps

Based on: an interview broadcast by CNN (Erin Burnett OutFront).

🎧 Listen to Reading 1
Click play to listen

In his first television interview since his arrest, U.S. Air Force Major Jason Watson, an officer with seventeen years of service, explained why he had stood on the steps of the U.S. Capitol in uniform and called for the impeachment and removal of President Trump and Vice President Vance. Since then, the interviewer noted, the military has placed him under a gag order, restricted his movements and opened an investigation into his conduct.

Watson's case rests on a series of allegations: that the President has flagrantly violated the Constitution, broken the law and engaged in corruption, and that the war with Iran is an aggressive war of choice. In his view, many Americans have simply normalized this conduct. He had chosen the pseudonym “just a nobody”, he said, to show that you do not have to be anybody special to act. His slogan was blunt: impeach, convict, remove.

The interviewer pressed him on the personal cost. A court-martial could end his career and lead to the loss of his pay and benefits, even to prison. Watson did not dispute any of this. Nothing worth doing, he replied, can be done for free. Without sacrifice, protest is just words, and words are easy to ignore. He framed his decision as loyalty to his oath to the Constitution rather than to any individual.

On Iran, Watson contended that the consequences, including the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, had been strategically predictable, and suggested that the President had probably been advised of them. He drew a comparison with the war in Ukraine: if another country had launched such a war, he argued, Americans would look to their allies and ask what could be done about it.

Supporters are likely to see an officer of conscience; critics, an officer who has breached the political neutrality on which civilian control of the military depends. Either way, the case raises a question every lawyer will recognise: where does an individual's right to speak end, and an institution's right to discipline begin?

🔑 Teacher note. Pause after paragraph 2 and ask: which statements are facts (arrest, 17 years of service, gag order) and which are allegations (violating the Constitution, corruption)? This prepares the “Read Like a Lawyer” section.

03 Reading 1B · Counsel’s Opinion: What Charges Could Follow?

🎧 Listen to Reading 1B
Click play to listen

The network then turned to its legal analyst, Ryan Goodman, a former special counsel at the Department of Defense. His first point was historical. Public dissent by an active-duty officer against a sitting president, he said, is extremely rare, and he could recall only two modern precedents.

During President Clinton's impeachment, some officers who had criticised him online were immediately warned by their superiors to stop or face a court-martial. During the Vietnam War, a second lieutenant who carried a placard condemning what he called President Johnson's fascist aggression was court-martialled and convicted.

Goodman said he could think of four charges the military might bring, and he gave examples. The first is using contemptuous words against the President, the offence in the Vietnam-era case. Others fall under failure to obey orders and regulations: service members may not speak at a political gathering, and may not attend one in uniform. On the analyst's account, Watson did both.

The military's dilemma, he explained, is deterrence. If it does nothing, others may be tempted to follow. Yet a high-profile court-martial could give the officer an even larger platform. A quieter route would be administrative: an other-than-honourable discharge, which would remove him from the service and could cost him his pension, without handing him a courtroom stage.

Finally, the analyst pointed to a distinction that runs through the whole story. What uniformed commanders consider the right response, and what the political leadership at the Department of Defense or the White House wants, may not be the same thing.

⚖️ The legal sources behind the analysis
Article 88 of the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ) punishes commissioned officers who use contemptuous words against the President and other senior officials. Article 92 covers failure to obey an order or regulation. Department of Defense Directive 1344.10 sets out which political activities service members may and may not engage in, including the ban on attending political events in uniform. The Vietnam-era case is United States v. Howe (1967).

04 Reading 2 · Leverage in the Strait

Based on: a White House report broadcast by One America News (OAN).

🎧 Listen to Reading 2
Click play to listen

In a separate report broadcast from the White House by One America News, correspondent Daniel Baldwin described a shift in tone. The President had written on Truth Social that Iran wanted to make a deal quickly and badly, adding that he alone would determine whether the United States would engage, a concept to which, he said, Washington was open. He had also predicted that the conflict would end very soon, probably right after the midterms, accusing Iran of holding out to complicate the election.

The correspondent's reading was that the President was keeping his options open: not pushing for a deal, but not closing the door on one either. In the reporter's view, this posture reflects a belief that Washington holds significant leverage. Much of that leverage, the report said, comes from a U.S. naval blockade. According to Central Command, U.S. forces have redirected more than one hundred commercial vessels to ensure compliance, and the President insists that oil is once again flowing through the Strait of Hormuz.

The President also argued that other countries, which he said had given no help whatsoever, should and would reimburse the United States once the conflict was over. The report recalled that he had previously floated the idea of staying and keeping the oil, as he said had happened with Venezuela, although other people had advised against it. Under the arrangement with Venezuela, the correspondent said, the United States gained majority control of more than sixty-five million barrels of oil reserves.

On prices, the President blamed increases on the previous administration and predicted that oil would drop like a rock once the fighting ended. The report cited motoring association figures of about four dollars and thirty-one cents per gallon of gasoline. It also noted that a meeting in Oman between Gulf countries and Iran on the Strait of Hormuz had been postponed. The situation, the correspondent concluded, remained very fluid.

🔑 Teacher note. Energy-sector link: ask the student what the redirection of commercial vessels would mean for an ENI-type operator — delivery obligations, demurrage, war-risk premiums, notices under force-majeure clauses. This sets up Exercise 7.

05 Read Like a Lawyer: Claims, Facts and Stance

Almost every sentence in both reports relays what someone said. A lawyer’s first instinct is to separate the fact of the statement from the truth of its content. The reporting verb you choose tells your reader how much weight to give a claim, so choose it deliberately.

Verb / phraseWhat it signalsExample from the lesson
allegestates something is true without proof yetWatson alleges that the President has broken the law.
contend / maintainargues a position, often against oppositionHe contends the war was strategically predictable.
assert / insiststates strongly and confidentlyThe President insists that oil is flowing again.
predictsays something will happenHe predicts the conflict will end after the midterms.
according toattributes information to a sourceAccording to Central Command, over 100 vessels were redirected.
reportedly / allegedlysignals that the writer is not vouching for the claimThe meeting was reportedly postponed.
Stance signals in the CNN report
The interviewer closes by thanking the officer “for speaking out” and insists that he is “a somebody”, a framing that is sympathetic to his action.
Stance signals in the OAN report
The correspondent adopts the President’s framing of leverage and relays his posts at length with little challenge. The clip ends with a promotion for the channel’s own subscription service.
⚖️ The constitutional question underneath
Watson calls the war unconstitutional. Under Article I, Section 8, Congress has the power to declare war; under Article II, Section 2, the President is Commander-in-Chief. The War Powers Resolution (1973) requires the President to report to Congress within 48 hours of introducing forces into hostilities and to withdraw them within 60 days (extendable by 30) unless Congress authorises the action. Where exactly the line falls has been disputed by successive administrations of both parties, and neither report sets out the administration’s legal position.

07 Cultural & Legal Context

🏛️ Impeach, convict, remove
Watson’s three-verb slogan follows the U.S. procedure exactly. The House impeaches by simple majority; the Senate tries the case and convicts only with a two-thirds majority; conviction means removal. Grounds under Article II, Section 4: “treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors.”
🎖️ Civilian control of the military
The U.S. system rests on an apolitical military under elected civilian authority. That is why rules on political activity in uniform are strict, and why the analyst called Watson’s action almost unprecedented.
🇮🇹 A comparison with Italy
Italy has similar principles: the Codice dell’ordinamento militare (D.Lgs. 66/2010) restricts political activity by military personnel, including participation in political events in uniform. The underlying idea, neutrality of the armed forces, is shared by most democracies.
🗳️ Midterms
Midterm elections fall halfway through a presidential term. All 435 House seats and about a third of the Senate are contested, so they often act as a referendum on the President.
🛢️ Why Hormuz matters
Roughly a fifth of the oil consumed worldwide passes through the Strait of Hormuz, which is only about 33 km wide at its narrowest point. Any disruption moves global prices within hours.
📜 Force majeure in practice
Blockades, war and government action are classic force-majeure events, but the clause decides everything: the list of events, the notice period, the duty to mitigate, and what happens if the event lasts beyond a set number of days.

08 Myth vs Reality

Click each card to reveal the reality.

MYTHMembers of the armed forces give up their right to free speech.
REALITYThey keep their First Amendment rights, but U.S. courts accept tighter limits for the military. In Parker v. Levy (1974) the Supreme Court described the military as a specialised society separate from civilian society. Private political opinions are generally allowed; speaking at a political rally, or attending one in uniform, is not.
MYTHIf the President is impeached, he is removed from office.
REALITYImpeachment is only the charge, brought by the House by simple majority. Removal requires conviction by two-thirds of the Senate. To date, no U.S. president has ever been removed by the Senate.
MYTHA court-martial is the only way to deal with a disobedient officer.
REALITYThe analyst's point was precisely the opposite: an administrative discharge can remove an officer, with serious financial consequences, without a public trial.
MYTHA naval blockade is just a policy tool with no special legal meaning.
REALITYIn international law, a blockade is traditionally a method of warfare. The San Remo Manual (1994) describes the conditions usually required: it must be declared and notified, effective, and applied impartially to vessels of all states.
MYTHIf a broadcaster reports a claim, the claim has been verified.
REALITYBoth reports mostly relay what people said or wrote. A careful reader separates the fact of the statement from the truth of its content, exactly as a lawyer does with evidence.

Exercise 8

Sample (Task A): Two recent broadcasts shed light on the U.S.–Iran conflict. According to CNN, an Air Force major was arrested after calling, in uniform, for the President’s impeachment; he alleges unconstitutional conduct and faces possible charges under the UCMJ. OAN, by contrast, reports that the President is “open to” negotiations and maintains that a U.S. naval blockade gives Washington decisive leverage.
